33 Edible Thistle

Names

Common name – Edible Thistle

Scientific name – Cirsium edule

Other names – ts’k’lawsxn; Indian Thistle

Fig 33.1: Edible Thistle ( Cirsium edule ) displaying its characteristic pinkish-purple flower head and prominent spines.

Fig 33.2: A close-up view of Edible Thistle, highlighting the tall perennial structure with prominent hairy bracts.

General information

Edible Thistle ( Cirsium edule ) is a tall perennial plant native to North America. [1, 9] Edible Thistle represents a significant traditional medicine with well-documented Indigenous uses and validated pharmacological properties. [2, 3] Its rich phytochemical profile, including flavonoids, phenolic compounds, triterpenoids, and polyacetylenes, provides the biochemical foundation for its traditional applications in treating inflammation, supporting liver health, and promoting wound healing. [2] The traditional knowledge of Indigenous peoples regarding this plant’s medicinal properties aligns closely with modern scientific understanding of its bioactive compounds and their mechanisms of action. [2, 5] This convergence of traditional wisdom and scientific validation underscores the importance of preserving and respecting Indigenous botanical knowledge. [5]

Physical Characteristics:

  • Height: 1-2 meters tall
  • Spread: 25-50 cm
  • Flowers: Pinkish-purple, prominent
  • Features: Tall perennial with prominent spines and hairy bracts
  • Habitat: Common in wet meadows and forest openings [1, 9]

Traditional Indigenous Uses

The people used its roots and leaves to treat swollen joints and stiffness in the body. [5] Decoctions made from the roots and poultices of the leaves brought relief to those burdened by arthritis or long days of labour. [5] The thistle’s medicine worked by drawing out the heat and swelling. [5]

When the body was wounded or burned, the green parts of the thistle were crushed or pressed to release their juice, which was applied directly to the skin. [5] This helped cuts and burns to close and heal cleanly. [5] The plant was also known to quiet the liver and ease digestion. [5] Teas made from the roots supported the inner organs, helping the body cleanse itself after times of illness or scarcity. [5] The same infusions could help with kidney troubles and fluid retention, its mild diuretic action allowing the body to release what it no longer needed. [5] When bleeding occurred, crushed thistle was pressed to the wound to stop the flow. [5]

The roots and young shoots were cooked and eaten as food in early spring, providing strength and vitamins when the winter stores were nearly gone. [1, 5, 9] Some said its energy supported the heart, improving circulation and vitality. [5]

Phytochemical Composition

Primary Bioactive Compound Classes

  1. Flavonoids

The genus Cirsium contains significant flavonoid compounds that contribute to its medicinal properties. [2, 7]

Major Flavonoids Identified:

  • Cirsimaritin (4′,5,6-trihydroxy-7-methoxyflavone)
  • Hispidulin (4′,5-dihydroxy-7,8-dimethoxyflavone)
  • Cirsimarin (flavonoid glycoside)
  • Linarin (acacetin-7-O-β-D-glucoside)

Biochemical Mechanisms of Action

  1. Anti-inflammatory Activity

Primary Mechanism:

  • Inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2) enzymes [2, 3]
  • Reduction of prostaglandin synthesis [2, 3]
  • Modulation of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) [2, 3]

Chemical Reaction Pathway:

Arachidonic Acid + COX-1/COX-2 → Prostaglandin H₂ → Inflammatory Prostaglandins ↑ (Inhibited by flavonoids)

Flavonoid-OH + •OH → Flavonoid-O• + H₂O

(Free radical scavenging reaction)

  1. Antioxidant Activity

Mechanism:

  • Free radical scavenging through phenolic hydroxyl groups [2, 3]
  • Metal ion chelation preventing oxidative damage [2, 3]
  • Enhancement of endogenous antioxidant systems [2, 3]

Antioxidant Reactions:

DPPH• + Phenolic-OH → DPPH-H + Phenolic-O•

(Stable radical formation)

Fe³⁺ + Flavonoid → Fe²⁺-Flavonoid Complex

(Metal chelation reaction)

  1. Hepatoprotective Activity

Mechanism:

  • Protection against oxidative liver damage [2]
  • Enhancement of liver detoxification enzymes [2]
  • Maintenance of hepatocyte membrane integrity [2]

Biochemical Pathway:

Hepatotoxin → Oxidative Stress → Lipid Peroxidation ↑  (Blocked by antioxidants)

GSH + ROS → GSSG + H₂O

(Glutathione-mediated protection enhanced by plant compounds)

  1. Antimicrobial Activity

Mechanism:

  • Disruption of microbial cell membranes [2, 8]
  • Inhibition of microbial enzymes [2, 8]
  • DNA binding and replication interference [2, 8]

Antimicrobial Reaction:

Polyacetylene + Bacterial Membrane → Membrane Disruption

Phenolic Compounds + Bacterial Proteins → Protein Denaturation

Pharmacological Validation

  1. Anti-inflammatory: Confirmed through COX inhibition assays [2, 3]
  2. Antioxidant: DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging validated [2, 3]
  3. Hepatoprotective: Demonstrated in liver injury models [2]
  4. Antimicrobial: Active against various bacterial and fungal strains [8]
  5. Cytotoxic: Selective activity against cancer cell lines [8]

Research Evidence: Several Cirsium species are known for their uses in traditional medicine and are studied for their phytochemical content and biological activities, showing cytotoxic activity with selectivity indices favoring cancer cells over normal cells. [8]

Traditional Preparation Methods

  1. Root Preparations:
    • Fresh roots harvested in fall
    • Boiled or roasted for consumption
    • Dried and powdered for medicine
  2. Leaf Applications:
    • Fresh leaves crushed for poultices
    • Dried leaves for teas and infusions
    • Steam preparations for respiratory conditions
  3. Whole Plant Extracts:
    • Water extractions for general medicine
    • Alcohol tinctures for concentrated preparations

Safety Profile and Contraindications

Safety Considerations:

  • Generally recognized as safe when used traditionally [5]
  • High fiber content may cause digestive upset in large quantities [1]
  • Spines require careful handling during harvesting [1, 9]

Potential Interactions:

  • May enhance effects of anti-inflammatory medications [2]
  • Possible interaction with blood-thinning agents [2]
  • Monitor blood sugar levels due to aldose reductase inhibition [6]

Conservation and Sustainable Use

  • Collect roots in late fall when nutrients are concentrated [1, 9]
  • Harvest only mature plants (2+ years old) [9]
  • Leave adequate population for regeneration [9]
  • Rotate harvesting areas to prevent overexploitation [9]

Habitat Requirements:

  • Wet meadows and forest openings [1, 9]
  • Rich, moist soils [1, 9]
  • Partial shade to full sun tolerance [1, 9]
