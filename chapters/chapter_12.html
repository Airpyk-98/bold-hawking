12 Stinging Nettle Tea

Names

Common Name – Stinging Nettle

Scientific Name – Urtica dioica

Other Names – Common Stinging Nettle, Burn Nettle, Nettle Leaf, Grande Ortie.

The young leaves can be eaten as greens and contain iron which can help treat anemia [3, 9] . The name stinging nettle comes from the tiny hairs on the plant that act like needle when touched [1] . The resulting pain is caused by neurotransmitters such as histamine, acetylcholine and serotonin, and by acidic components such as formic acid, tartaric acid and oxalic acid [1] .

Despite the pain caused by touching the plant, several parts of the stinging nettle can be used for medicinal purposes [7, 9] . Stinging nettle is rich in vitamins A, B, C, D and K and can be applied topically as an anti-inflammatory or to help treat arthritis [2, 9] . When consumed as a tea, it provides absorbable iron and polyphenols, again, demonstrating anti-inflammatory properties [3, 5] . Research has found that stinging nettles also contain terpenes, fatty acids, carotenes and carotenoids, polyphenolic compounds and amino acids amongst other compounds [3] . Many of these were also shown to be present in higher concentrations in mature leaves as opposed to young leaves [3] .

Traditional Indigenous Uses

It was one of the first greens to appear in the spring, pushing up through the soil when the snow had barely melted [4, 6] . The young leaves, gathered before the plant flowered, were cooked and eaten like spinach or made into soup to bring strength after the long winter [4, 6] . When brewed into a tea, nettle became a cleansing tonic, purifying the blood, strengthening the liver, and restoring energy to the body [4] . It was known to the people as a “spring cleanser,” helping to awaken the body and spirit [4] .

For women, it was used to ease labor pains, regulate menstruation, and prevent excessive bleeding after childbirth [6] . Mothers would drink nettle tea to strengthen the uterus and to help bring in milk after their babies were born [6] . Nettle supported the blood. Its tonic was used for anemia, to increase vitality, and to balance the body’s inner strength [4, 6] . The plant’s power extended to the kidneys and bladder, where it worked as a gentle diuretic to relieve urinary troubles and even help men with prostate issues [6, 8] .

Those who suffered from arthritis, sore muscles, or back pain used nettle both inside and out [4, 11] . The fresh stinging leaves, when brushed lightly on the skin, were believed to draw pain away from the joints and restore circulation [4] . Steamed leaves were made into poultices to soothe the body, and teas were taken to ease the inflammation within [6] . Nettle was also valued for respiratory troubles e.g. coughs, asthma, hay fever, and bronchial congestion were all treated with its warm infusion [4, 10] . On the outside, nettle was applied as a wash or poultice to heal wounds, burns, eczema, and other skin irritations [4, 11] . Some used the rinse for their hair to strengthen it and prevent loss [4] . The plant’s fibers, drawn from the long stems, were used for weaving rope, nets, and cloth [6] .

Preparation and Safety Considerations

Tea Preparation

  • Use dried leaves (stinging compounds neutralized) or carefully blanched fresh leaves [7]
  • Steep 1-2 teaspoons dried herb per cup in hot water (80-90°C) [7]
  • Infuse 10-15 minutes for optimal extraction [7]
  • Can be consumed 2-3 times daily [7]

Safety Notes

  • Fresh plant causes dermal irritation; always cook, dry, or blanch before internal use [1]
  • High vitamin K content may interfere with anticoagulant medications (warfarin) [9]
  • May lower blood pressure and blood sugar (monitor if on medications) [9]
  • Generally safe during pregnancy based on traditional use, but consult healthcare provider [4, 6]
  • Root extracts should be reserved for specific prostate indications [2, 8]
